On weather and still water

The Pond
in the Rain

A mirror, a squall, and the mirror again: what the wind and each drop do to the water, why the water goes grey, and what the sun finds after.

Still water under a bank of trees is the most exact mirror most of us will ever see out of doors. Stand at the edge and look across, and the willows on the far side hang upside down beneath themselves, every trunk carried on below the waterline, the sky laid in pale between the branches. You are looking at a glancing angle, and at a glancing angle water is a far better mirror than it seems from above. Straight down it gives back about two parts in a hundred and you see the mud; toward the far bank more than half of the light that reaches your eye has come off the surface. Then the first drop lands.

Wind first

A summer shower announces itself on water before it arrives. The light goes first: the bright gaps of sky between the trees turn to slate, and the mirror, which can only give back what it is shown, goes dull with them. Then, far off across the lake, a grey wall with its foot on the water: the rain itself, seen side on, leaning a little with the wind that drives it, swallowing the far trees one after another. Just ahead of it comes the gust. You can watch it cross the pond as dark patches racing over the surface, the ones sailors call cat’s paws. Each is a patch of ripples a few millimetres high, too small to see one by one, but enough to break the mirror where they pass, so that the water there shows its own dark instead of the sky. The willows lift and toss all at once, the long switches at the top of the crowns most of all.

The first drops

The first drops of a shower are usually the largest. Big drops fall faster than small ones, close to nine metres a second against two for drizzle, so they outrun the rest of the cloud’s rain and arrive on their own, a few to a pond, each one a sound you can place. A drop four millimetres across punches a crater several times its own size, throws a thin crown up round the rim, and then the crater fills from below so fast that the water overshoots and leaps up the middle as a column. Arthur Worthington drew this by the light of an electric spark in the 1870s and photographed it in the 1890s; his book of the pictures, A Study of Splashes, came out in 1908, and the column still carries his name. Often its tip pinches off as a single droplet, which rises a little further, falls back, and makes a second, smaller ring inside the first.

A train of rings

What spreads from the crater is never one ring but a train of them. On water the shortest ripples, held taut by surface tension, travel faster than the longer waves behind them, so the finest crests run out ahead and the train lengthens as it goes, smoothing as it spreads. They widen by twenty or thirty centimetres a second, and within a second or two viscosity has taken them out altogether. Rings from different drops pass straight through one another and come out unchanged on the other side, which is why a pond in light rain looks like a net of circles rather than a muddle.

Seen across the water they are not circles at all but flat ellipses, and you see them less as bumps than as bends in the reflection. Each crest tilts a strip of the mirror toward you or away, so a ring shows as a curved band where the picture of the trees is pushed up into the sky and pulled down into the trunks. Near the far bank, where the angle is lowest, a tilt of a degree or two is enough to swap the reflection of a leaf for the reflection of a cloud.

A pond in rain is not wetter. It is only rougher, and roughness is grey.

Why the pond goes grey

In a steady shower hundreds of drops land on every square metre every second, and the rings never get the chance to be seen one at a time. The surface becomes a fine, restless roughness, every few millimetres tilted a different way, and each point of it now reflects not one place on the bank but a scatter of them: a little sky, a little trunk, a little dark water under the leaves. The eye averages that into grey. The sharp edges go first, then the shapes, then the difference between the bright gaps and the dark trees. Under the rain cloud the sky is darker too, and a rough surface gives back the average of it rather than its brightest parts, so the pond darkens as it greys. The rain falling between you and the far bank veils the willows a little, and against their leaves, if you look, you can just make out the streaks.

The sound of it

You can hear the size of the rain. Drizzle on a pond is almost silent. Drops of about a millimetre make the hiss we think of as the sound of rain, and the hiss is not the impact at all: as the little crater closes it pinches off a tiny bubble at its floor, and the bubble, shuddering into its round shape, rings at around fourteen thousand cycles a second for a few thousandths of a second. Hundreds of them together are the hiss. Bigger drops trap bubbles only now and then, larger and lower, which is the plink and plop of a heavy shower, and the largest of all, the drops that collect on leaves and fall from the ends of branches, land with a single deep plunk you can hear from the other side of the water.

After

When the shower moves on, the trees go on raining for a while. Water that has run along the leaves gathers at their tips and at the low points of the branches and falls from the same places again and again, a fat drop every second or two at first, then every five, then every ten as the leaves drain. They make the biggest rings of all, close in under the bank, and between them the surface quiets. The fine roughness is the first thing to go, a few seconds after the last drop, and the reflection comes back out of the grey all at once: the trunks, the bright gaps of sky, the line of the far shore. Then the colour, which seems to have been rinsed. The willows are greener than they were before, and the water is holding them still again. If the sun comes through behind the last of the cloud, its reflection is not one image but a path of glints, each a small wave tilted just so to send the sun into your eye: narrow and close-packed far out, breaking into separate sparks as it comes toward you.
