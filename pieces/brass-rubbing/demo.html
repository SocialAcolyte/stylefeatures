In the chancel at Stoke d’Abernon, in Surrey, a knight lies in the floor under a sheet of metal as long as he was. He is Sir John d’Aubernoun, in mail from crown to toe, his lance against his shoulder and a lion under his feet biting its shaft, and his was long called the oldest brass in England. Brasses like his were cut from latten, a yellow alloy of copper and zinc beaten into plates in the Low Countries and engraved in London with a burin. The figure was laid in a hollow chiselled to its outline in a slab of Purbeck marble, bedded in pitch and pinned with rivets, and the letters of the epitaph were set round the edge one at a time: Sire Iohan Daubernoun chivaler gist icy, Deu de sa alme eyt mercy. Here lies Sir John d’Aubernoun, knight; God have mercy on his soul.

To take a rubbing you sweep the slab, lay a length of thin white paper over it and tape it down at the corners, then run your fingertips over the paper until you can feel where the metal ends. The heelball is a cake of beeswax, tallow and lampblack that cobblers used to blacken the heels and welts of boots. You rub with its edge, lightly at first and then harder: it rides on the metal and turns the paper black over it, and wherever the burin cut a line the paper sinks a little into the groove and stays white.

The picture comes up the right way round, not mirrored as a print is, because the wax goes on the side of the paper you are looking at. Victorian antiquaries rubbed brasses by the hundred to study armour and dress; by the nineteen-sixties it was a craze, and the brass-rubbing centres sold sticks of gold heelball and rolls of black paper to tourists. So many knees and hands wore so many figures thin that most churches now keep their brasses under carpet and offer a replica instead.

In China the same thing was done a thousand years earlier, and wet. The paper is laid on the stone damp and tamped into every cut with a stiff brush, and when it is nearly dry it is patted with a pad of silk stuffed with cotton and charged with ink, so the face of the stone goes black and the carved strokes of the characters stand out white. It was how the hand of a great calligrapher travelled: the stele stayed in a temple courtyard in Xi’an, but its characters went everywhere in mounted albums, and collectors paid most for the oldest impressions, taken before frost and other rubbings had worn the strokes away. The earliest that survives, of an inscription in the emperor Taizong’s own hand, was found sealed in a cave at Dunhuang.
