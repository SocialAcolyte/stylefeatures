An essay on the ink plum

The Plum
in Winter

The first tree to flower, in the cold, on bare wood, and the painters who gave their lives to it.

IBefore the thaw

Of all the trees in a Chinese garden, the plum is the one that does not wait. It flowers in the last weeks of the lunar year, when the ponds are still skinned with ice and the willows are bare whips, and it flowers on bare wood: the leaves come weeks later, after the petals have fallen. On a still morning after snow you can find it before you see it, by a sweetness in the cold air that seems to come from nowhere, and then the tree itself, black and crooked, with white flowers sitting along its twigs as if the snow had settled there in small round drops and refused to melt.

The botanists call it Prunus mume, and it is less a plum than an apricot, a small tree of the hill country south of the Yangzi that was being grown for its sour fruit three thousand years ago. Salted and dried, the fruit seasoned food before there was vinegar. But the Chinese loved it for its flowers, and especially for their timing. A flower that opens in the snow is a flower that has not been told it is spring. It was taken as a figure for the scholar who keeps his principles in a bad reign, the one who is upright when nobody is watching and there is nothing to be gained.

The flower is small, two or three centimetres across: five round petals, white or palest pink, a cup of five red-brown sepals behind them, and a burst of long stamens tipped with pollen, thirty or forty of them, fanning out like the spokes of a wheel. The buds sit almost flat against the twig, singly or in pairs, and a single branch carries every age at once: hard beads still wrapped in their sepals, flowers half open like cupped hands, and flowers open so wide that the petals have begun to curl back.

IIThree friends of winter

The pine stays green, the bamboo bends under the snow and does not break, and the plum flowers in it. Together they were called the three friends of the cold season, and they were painted together on scrolls, carved together on inkstones and woven together into the borders of robes, a promise of company to anyone going through a hard winter of their own.

The plum had its saint. Lin Bu, a poet of the early eleventh century, refused every office he was offered and spent twenty years on Solitary Hill in the West Lake at Hangzhou, without ever, it was said, going into the city. He never married. He kept plum trees and two cranes, and people said he had taken the plum for his wife and the cranes for his children. When a guest came while he was out on the lake in his boat, the servant let the cranes go, and Lin Bu saw them circling over the hill and rowed home. Two lines of his about the trees at dusk became the most quoted lines ever written about a flower:

Sparse shadows lie aslant across the clear, shallow water;
a hidden fragrance drifts in the yellow moonlight of dusk.

Every later poet who wrote about the plum was writing in the shadow of those shadows. A century and a half later the poet Jiang Kui, asked for new songs on a snowy night, wrote two and called them simply An Xiang and Shu Ying, Hidden Fragrance and Sparse Shadows, and everybody knew where the words came from.

IIIA shadow on the window

Painters had painted the plum in colour for a long time, petal by petal in white and rouge, as the court painters painted peonies. The ink plum began, the story goes, with a monk. Zhongren lived at the Huaguang monastery in the south at the end of the eleventh century, and one night he saw the shadow of a flowering branch thrown by the moon onto the paper of his window. He took a brush and painted the shadow as he saw it, in ink alone, and found that it had everything: the twist of the old wood, the spring of the young shoots, the flowers as small pale shapes against the dark.

The man who made it an art was Yang Wujiu, who lived from 1097 to 1171 and refused to serve at court under a chief minister he despised. He painted almost nothing but plum. He drew the blossoms in outline, a ring of fine strokes for each petal with the paper left white inside, so that the flowers seem to give off their own light, and he painted the branches with a dry, fast brush that left the old wood rough and broken and the new shoots as straight as arrows. The story goes that the emperor, who liked his flowers in colour, called it village plum. Yang wrote on his next painting that it was a village plum painted by imperial command.

His most famous scroll, painted in his late sixties, shows the same branch four times: in bud, beginning to open, in full flower, and about to fall. He wrote four short songs at the end of it, one for each, and the bud comes first.

IVThe manuals

By the thirteenth century there were so many plum painters that the plum got its own handbooks. In 1238 a minor official named Song Boren, who kept plum trees at his house and said he had watched them for years, published the Manual of Plum Blossom Likenesses, one hundred woodcuts of single sprigs, each with a little poem beside it. It is the oldest printed picture book of its kind that survives. It follows the flower through its whole life in eight stages, from the tight bud, through the small bud and the swelling bud and the bud about to open, to the open flower, the flower in its full glory, the flower about to fall, and the fruit setting, and it gives each sprig a fanciful name for the thing its shape recalls.

In 1351 Wu Taisu, a scholar who signed himself the Master of the Pine Studio, wrote down the rules of the whole art in his Plum Manual. It was lost in China and survived only in copies kept in Japan, where it lay for six centuries. It tells the painter how to build a tree from the bottom up, the trunk first and the flowers last; that the old wood is painted with a dry brush and the young with a wet one; that branches should cross in the shape of the character for woman, never in a neat cross; that the young shoots must be straight and must not be many. It asks for flowers that face every way, some toward you, some away, some seen from the side, and never two alike.

An older rule among plum lovers says it shortest. The plum is prized for four things: for being sparse and not crowded, old and not young, lean and not plump, and closed rather than open. The best of it is the bud.

VA thousand trees

Wang Mian was a cowherd's son from Zhejiang who taught himself to read by slipping into the village school while the cattle grazed, and at night sat in the lap of a Buddha in the temple to read by the lamp kept burning there. He grew up to be the greatest plum painter of the fourteenth century and would not take office under the Mongols. In the end he went to live on a mountain near Kuaiji, planted a thousand plum trees, called his hut the House of Plum Blossom, and lived by trading his paintings for rice.

His branches hang down from the top of the scroll, heavy with flowers, as if the tree were growing out of the sky, and in among the dark old limbs the young shoots rise straight up, thin as the strokes of a character. That is how the tree grows. A plum that is left alone sends up long whips from its old wood every summer, and the next winter it flowers along them, so that the newest wood carries the most flowers. An old limb, cracked and grey with lichen, flowers on the shoots it put out last year.

The painters knew this, and they painted the order of it. You start with the oldest wood, in a dry brush heavy with ink, letting it break and drag. The twigs come next, each one a single stroke, made fast and wet so that it tapers and ends in a point. Then the flowers, a few strokes each, and last the dots: the moss on the old bark, the sepals, the black tips of the stamens. Done in that order, a painting grows the way the tree did.

In Japan the plum came before the cherry. In the oldest anthology of Japanese poetry there are well over a hundred poems about it and fewer than half as many about cherry blossom. When the minister Sugawara no Michizane was sent into exile in 901, he wrote to the plum tree in his garden: when the east wind blows, send me your scent, plum flowers; though your master is gone, do not forget the spring. The story is that the tree pulled up its roots and flew after him to Kyushu, where it is still growing, and still flowers first.
