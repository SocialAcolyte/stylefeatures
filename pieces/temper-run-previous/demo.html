A chisel straight out of the quench is as hard as steel gets and nearly as brittle as glass. The smith has brought it to a cherry red, eight hundred degrees or so, and plunged it into water; the carbon that was dissolved in the hot iron has been caught there, the crystals strained around it, and an edge ground on it now would chip at the first blow of the mallet. So it is let down. It is warmed again, gently, to somewhere between two and three hundred degrees, and at that heat some of the trapped carbon comes out as fine carbides, the strains relax, and the steel gives up a little of its hardness for a great deal of toughness.

The smith has no thermometer for this. He rubs one face of the chisel bright on a stone, puts the heat into the shank, and watches the bright face. As it warms the steel takes a skin of oxide, and the skin shows as colour, running down the blade ahead of the heat: a pale straw first, then a deeper straw, brown, a bronzy purple, blue. When the colour he wants reaches the cutting edge he quenches it again, and it stops.

The colours are not a stain or a pigment. The oxide is a film a few tens of millionths of a millimetre thick, thinner than a wavelength of light, and the colour is interference: light reflected from the top of the film and from the steel beneath it meet out of step, and some wavelengths cancel. A thicker film cancels longer ones, so the colour walks through the spectrum as the film grows, and the film grows faster the hotter the steel is. Time counts as well as heat: a long soak at a lower heat gives the same colour as a short one at a higher. Grind the face again and the colour is gone, but the temper stays.

Each colour has its trades. Pale straw, a little over two hundred degrees, is left on razors, scrapers and engravers, which must hold the finest edge and are never struck. The darker straws and browns go to chisels 0% and punches, purple to axes, and blue, near three hundred degrees, to springs and saws, which must bend and come back. Running the colours from the shank leaves a chisel graded along its length: hard at the edge, where it cuts, and softer and tougher behind it, where the mallet lands.
