In the summer of 1863, ill and kept to the house, Darwin set pots of climbing plants on the sill of his study window and watched them by the hour. The tip of a young passionflower is never still: it sweeps a slow ellipse, about once an hour, feeling for something to hold. The tendrils revolve as well, held out straight and stiff, and when one touches a stick it bends within half a minute and is soon wound fast. Then the free length coils into a spring that draws the stem in, turning one way for half its length and back the other way for the rest, because both its ends are held. The Spanish friars who first saw the flower read the Passion in it: the crown of thorns in the corona, three nails in the styles, five wounds in the anthers, and ten faithful apostles in its petals and sepals.
